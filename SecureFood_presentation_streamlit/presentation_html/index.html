Why Agent-Based Modelling for Food Policy?

Food policy is not a single average effect. It is a dynamic system of heterogeneous consumers, adaptive baskets, and cascading responses under shocks.

01
Equilibrium vs. reality Econometric models estimate average effects; food policy operates out of equilibrium, amid shocks and frictions.
02
Emergent behaviour ABM agents follow calibrated preference rules; macro patterns are not pre-programmed — they emerge.
03
Cascading responses Simulates the chain: scarcity → hoarding → substitution → waste, with heterogeneous agents.
04
Uncertainty quantified Outputs full distributions of outcomes, not a single point estimate. Essential for robust policy advice.

Calibration Evidence 1 | Willingness to Pay

Finnish origin is the dominant dairy premium.

What premium do Finnish consumers pay for origin, organic certification, and fat content?
Answer
Origin dominates; fat is penalised.
€2.68Finnish origin premium
€0.86Organic certification premium
-€0.85Penalty per 1% point higher fat content
Origin commands a premium roughly three times larger than organic certification. In this dairy setting, national origin is the strongest revealed-preference signal.
Mixed Logit model; Krinsky-Robb 95% confidence intervals; n = 116.
Calibration Evidence 2 | Inflation Shock

Income cohorts adapted in similar ways.

How do income cohorts adapt their dairy basket when prices rise 20%?
Answer
The adjustment pattern is budget-driven, not income-driven.
Consumers reduced basket intensity under the 20% price shock, but the changes were not statistically different across income cohorts.
n=116Participants observed across rounds
p>0.82Kruskal-Wallis tests across basket outcomes
20%Experimental price shock
The policy implication is important: income labels alone do not identify who changes behaviour under price pressure.
Bridge to simulation: observed basket adaptation looks similar, but agent-based stress exposure can still reveal distributional vulnerability.
Mean change from Round 1 to Round 2; 95% confidence intervals.

RQ1: Who Should Be Protected First Under a 20% Price Shock?

No policy → purchase cap + calming
Answer

Low-income consumers are the first protection priority.

In the no-policy shock run, low-income consumers show the highest budget-exhaustion pressure. A simple rationing and calming communication response reduces that pressure substantially.

0.39%low-income budget-exhaustion vulnerability index, no policy
0.06%vulnerability index after purchase cap plus calming communication
Policy reading The first response should protect access and dampen panic-driven pressure before broader market interventions are evaluated.

RQ2: How Does a Fat Tax Shift Dairy Demand?

Tax target → observed substitution
Answer

The tax suppresses high-fat demand more than it creates substitution.

A 20% tax on products with fat content at or above 3.5% cuts high-fat product sales sharply. Lower-fat products absorb only a very small increase, suggesting partial avoidance rather than complete switching.

-48.4%high-fat product sales under isolated tax run
+0.7%lower-fat product sales
Policy reading A fat tax changes the targeted category clearly, but additional measures may be needed if the objective is active substitution toward healthier dairy baskets.

RQ3: Does the Market Self-Correct After an Autumn Dairy Shock?

Shock window → recovery trace
Answer

The system partially recovers, but the shock leaves a measurable trace.

Fulfilment improves once the crisis window closes, but does not return to the pre-shock state. The policy bundle reduces volatility, yet it does not fully eliminate disruption effects.

16.4%mean order-fulfilment index during shock, no response
18.6%mean order-fulfilment index during recovery, no response
Policy reading The market shows recovery capacity, but policy is still needed to reduce volatility and protect access during the disruption window.

What the Framework Proves

The ABM answers the policy questions. The broader contribution is the infrastructure: revealed consumer behaviour can be collected remotely, calibrated into agents, and reused for stress-testing before policy reaches households.

🌐
No Lab Required
Participants shop from home in any browser. 116 Finnish consumers were recruited in 2 days, with zero lab infrastructure needed.
🛒
Revealed, Not Stated
Actual product selections and basket decisions — not hypothetical answers. The gap between what people say and what they do disappears.
🌍
Globally Scalable
The same protocol deploys in Finland, Germany, or Greece with a URL. Cross-country and longitudinal replications become trivially cheap.
116 participants  ·  18 DCE tasks each  ·  2 shopping rounds  ·  ~30 min total
